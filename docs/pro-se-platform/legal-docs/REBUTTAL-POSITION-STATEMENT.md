## PRELIMINARY STATEMENT

Charging Party Marc Castillo submits this Rebuttal to Respondent's Position Statement dated October 16, 2025. The documentary record—including Respondent's own exhibits—establishes that Respondent's stated justifications are contradicted by contemporaneous documents, that the causal chain between protected activity and adverse action is unbroken, and that Respondent engaged in spoliation of electronically stored information after the duty to preserve attached.

This Rebuttal proceeds in reverse chronological order to demonstrate that each adverse action was the proximate result of prior protected activity, and that each stated justification is either (a) contradicted by Respondent's own records, (b) temporally impossible, or (c) both.

**Causation Standards Applied:**
- ADA/Title VII Retaliation: But-for causation. *Univ. of Tex. Sw. Med. Ctr. v. Nassar*, 570 U.S. 338 (2013).
- FMLA Retaliation: But-for causation. 29 U.S.C. § 2615(a)(2).
- SOX Whistleblower: Contributing factor with burden shift. *Murray v. UBS Sec., LLC*, 601 U.S. 23 (2024).

## I. EVIDENCE DESTRUCTION AND ADVERSE INFERENCE (SEPTEMBER 2025)

### A. Spoliation of Electronically Stored Information

**December 16, 2024 – Request to Delete Electronic Communications.** On December 16, 2024, eight days after Charging Party self-emailed documentation to his personal address for preservation (December 8, 2024), Charging Party's manager transmitted a text message requesting deletion of email communications (CAST-MGR-0023). Whether the temporal proximity between preservation and deletion request establishes anticipatory spoliation presents a threshold determination under the duty to preserve. But for the deletion request, the electronic communications would have remained in their native location subject to ordinary retention policies. *Zubulake v. UBS Warburg LLC*, 220 F.R.D. 212, 216 (S.D.N.Y. 2003) (holding duty to preserve arises when party knows or reasonably should know that evidence is relevant to pending or reasonably foreseeable litigation).

**May 12, 2025 – Simultaneous Unauthorized Access and Preservation Request Cancellation.** On May 12, 2025, Zscaler access logs recorded simultaneous unauthorized login attempts to Charging Party's account from multiple geographic locations, triggering forced logout protocols (CAST-CYBER-0001). On the same date, Respondent's IT department canceled pending preservation requests for access logs and authentication records (CAST-HR-0018). Whether the cancellation of preservation requests during active unauthorized access constitutes intentional deprivation of evidence requires application of Fed. R. Civ. P. 37(e)(2). But for the cancellation, the access logs would have documented the identity and authorization status of each login attempt. *Residential Funding Corp. v. DeGeorge Fin. Corp.*, 306 F.3d 99, 107 (2d Cir. 2002) (finding intentional destruction where party acted with knowledge that evidence was relevant to ongoing dispute).

**September 8, 2025 – Email Account Deletion One Day Before Mediation.** On September 8, 2025, one day before the EEOC mediation scheduled for September 9, 2025, attempts to contact marc.castillo@schwab.com returned SMTP error code 550 5.1.1 "User Unknown" (CAST-CYBER-0002). The account had processed incoming mail without error on July 31, 2025. Whether deletion of the primary evidence repository one day before mediation demonstrates intent to deprive under Fed. R. Civ. P. 37(e)(2) follows from the temporal sequence. But for the deletion or transfer to cold storage, the email account would have remained accessible for production in response to EEOC document requests. *Zubulake*, 220 F.R.D. at 220.

**Application of Adverse Inference Standard.** Fed. R. Civ. P. 37(e)(2) permits sanctions including adverse inference where a party acts with intent to deprive another party of the information's use in litigation. The sequence of preservation (December 8, 2024), deletion request (December 16, 2024), cancellation of preservation protocols during unauthorized access (May 12, 2025), and account deletion one day before mediation (September 8, 2025) establishes the elements of Rule 37(e)(2). Whether Respondent acted with intent to deprive is answered by the temporal pattern and the complete omission of these events from Respondent's Position Statement. But for the intentional destruction, the electronically stored information would have been available to test Respondent's employment defenses. *Residential Funding*, 306 F.3d at 108.

## II. TERMINATION DURING EEOC MEDIATION (AUGUST 2025)

**August 11, 2025 – EEOC Mediation Acceptance and Immediate Employer Action.** On August 11, 2025, Respondent accepted the EEOC's mediation invitation. Two business days later, on August 13, 2025, Respondent revised termination letter verbiage and timeline—documents demonstrating deliberate modification of employment action concurrent with pending mediation (CAST-TRM-0002). Whether an employer may unilaterally execute termination while affirmatively participating in EEOC alternative dispute resolution is the material question. But for Charging Party's invocation of EEOC processes, no rational basis exists for altering termination documentation during the precise window when settlement discussions were being calendared. *Burlington N. & Santa Fe Ry. Co. v. White*, 548 U.S. 53, 67-68 (2006) (adverse employment actions include conduct that "might have dissuaded a reasonable worker from making or supporting a charge of discrimination").

**August 14, 2025 – Respondent's Legal Communication Infrastructure Rendered Inoperative.** On August 14, 2025, Respondent's legal mailbox auto-reply declared the address "not monitored" and redirected correspondence to alternative departments (CAST-HR-0019). This occurred three days after mediation acceptance and one business day after termination document revisions. Whether an employer may disable formal legal communication channels while simultaneously processing termination and mediation obligations presents a procedural anomaly. *Nassar*, 570 U.S. at 360, requires that retaliation be a but-for cause of the adverse action.

**August 19, 2025 – Termination Execution During Active Mediation Coordination.** On August 19, 2025, Respondent executed termination via case HRC1296140—eight calendar days after accepting EEOC mediation (CAST-TRM-0003). That same day, EEOC Mediator Lorna Steuer communicated that Respondent's outside counsel would provide mediation response by month-end, yet Respondent proceeded with immediate employment severance. Charging Party's contemporaneous inquiry—"Is this Schwab's answer to Mediation?"—reflects the temporal incongruity. Whether termination during the mediation intake period constitutes retaliation depends on whether participation in protected activity was a but-for cause of the timing. But for the August 11 mediation acceptance, no coherent business rationale explains why an employer simultaneously (a) commits to good-faith settlement discussions through counsel, and (b) executes the precise employment action forming the subject matter of those discussions. *Burlington*, 548 U.S. at 70.

**August 22-25, 2025 – Medical Escalation Concurrent with Termination.** Between August 22-25, 2025, Charging Party's treating physicians escalated psychotropic medication dosages (Sertraline 100mg→150mg; Propranolol 20mg→60mg) in direct response to termination-induced psychiatric decompensation (CAST-MED-0024, CAST-MED-0025). This occurred within the same week as termination. Whether adverse employment action caused material harm is determined by objective medical intervention, not subjective complaint. But for the August 19 termination during active EEOC proceedings, no medical necessity required doubling beta-blocker dosage within 72 hours. *Nassar*, 570 U.S. at 362.

## III. SHIFTING TERMINATION RATIONALES / PRETEXT (JULY–AUGUST 2025)

**July 22, 2025 – Initial Termination Threat Without Acknowledgment of Approved Leave.** On July 22, 2025, Respondent issued its first termination-threat letter (CAST-TRM-0001), asserting that Charging Party had been absent since April 17, 2025, and demanding medical documentation by July 29, 2025. This assertion directly contradicts Respondent's own records: Sedgwick had already approved Charging Party's medical leave from January 21, 2025, through March 3, 2025 (CAST-SED-0010), and extended that approval through June 30, 2025 (CAST-SED-0039). Whether a termination threat based on alleged lack of documentation—when approved documentation already existed in Respondent's possession—evidences pretext is dispositive. *Reeves v. Sanderson Plumbing Prods., Inc.*, 530 U.S. 133, 147 (2000) (holding that a factfinder may infer discrimination from the falsity of the employer's explanation). But for Respondent's retaliatory animus following Charging Party's EEOC charge, Respondent would not have ignored its own third-party administrator's approval and threatened termination based on a fabricated absence of documentation. *Forrester v. Rauland-Borg Corp.*, 453 F.3d 416, 419 (7th Cir. 2006) ("Inconsistencies and shifting explanations can themselves be evidence of pretext").

**July 31, 2025 – Same Author, Same Date, Contradictory Statements.** On July 31, 2025, Kay Bristow authored two letters with irreconcilable positions. In one letter (CAST-ACC-0013), Bristow expressly acknowledged that Sedgwick had approved Charging Party's leave from April 17 through June 30, 2025. In the second letter issued the same day (Exhibit 17 in Respondent's Position Statement), Bristow extended the documentation deadline to August 7, 2025, and claimed that Charging Party had failed to provide required documentation. This same-day contradiction demonstrates that Respondent's stated reason—lack of documentation—was known to be false by the very individual asserting it. Whether an employer's simultaneous acknowledgment and denial of approved leave, by the same decision-maker on the same date, constitutes direct evidence of pretext under governing law is not reasonably disputable. *Zaccagnini v. Charles Levy Circulating Co.*, 338 F.3d 672, 676 (7th Cir. 2003) ("Shifting explanations for an employment decision can be evidence of pretext, particularly when the explanations are internally contradictory").

**August 13, 2025 – Third Deadline Extension and Continued False Narrative.** On August 13, 2025, Respondent issued a third letter (Exhibit 18) moving the documentation deadline to August 15, 2025, and setting termination for August 18, 2025 (CAST-TRM-0002). This third iteration continued to assert that Charging Party had not provided documentation supporting an ongoing need for leave—a claim Respondent knew to be false based on Sedgwick's January 21 and June 3, 2025, approval letters and Bristow's own July 31 acknowledgment. Whether three sequential letters with shifting deadlines and a consistently false justification—contradicted by Respondent's own exhibits—establish pretext as a matter of law is resolved by *Reeves*, 530 U.S. at 143 (permitting inference of discrimination from disbelief of employer's proffered reason).

**Respondent's Position Statement Contradicts Respondent's Own Exhibits.** Respondent's Position Statement (Page 7) asserts that "Castillo did not provide any documentation supporting an ongoing need for a leave of absence." This statement is flatly contradicted by Respondent's own Exhibit 17 (July 31, 2025), in which Kay Bristow acknowledges Sedgwick-approved leave through June 30, 2025. Whether an employer's reliance on a factually false rationale—disproven by its own exhibits submitted to the EEOC—establishes pretext is resolved by *Reeves*, 530 U.S. at 147.

## IV. FORCED LEAVE AND CONSTRUCTIVE DISCHARGE (APRIL–MAY 2025)

**April 16, 2025 – The Ultimatum and Respondent's Own Admission.** On April 16, 2025, Taylor Huffner (Benefits) presented Charging Party with a binary choice: work without accommodation or accept forced leave (CAST-REC-0002, audio recording). Respondent's Position Statement (Page 6) selectively quotes Charging Party as stating "I don't think I am capable of doing the current role." Respondent omits the predicate: this statement was made in response to being asked whether he could work WITHOUT the accommodation he had requested.

**The same day, Respondent's own email to Sedgwick (CAST-SED-0024) stated: "Marc cannot perform his role due to his need for accommodation. Place him back on leave."** Recipients included Accommodations@schwab.com, charlesschwab@sedgwick.com, Keenan Willis, Jonathan Carlisle, Cindy Baldwin, Kay Bristow, and Chrystal Hicks. This is an admission that (1) Respondent acknowledged Charging Party needed accommodation, (2) Respondent acknowledged he could not perform WITHOUT that accommodation, and (3) instead of providing accommodation, Respondent removed him from the workplace.

Whether this constitutes constructive discharge under *Pennsylvania State Police v. Suders*, 542 U.S. 129, 147 (2004), is resolved by the three-element test: (1) intolerable conditions, (2) employer's actions would cause reasonable person to resign, (3) official act precipitating resignation. The April 16 ultimatum—documented in Respondent's own email—was the official act. But for Respondent's refusal to provide accommodation, Charging Party would not have been forced onto leave.

**Medical Documentation of Intolerable Conditions.** The medical records establish objectively intolerable working conditions:
- April 10, 2025: BP 167/105 mmHg during manager interaction (CAST-MED-0017) — Stage 2 hypertensive crisis
- April 14, 2025: BP 164/102 mmHg post-accommodation cancellation (CAST-MED-0018)
- May 5, 2025: BP 140/105 mmHg despite Propranolol addition (CAST-MED-0020)

Whether these conditions were intolerable is not subjective—the medical records document life-threatening cardiovascular stress directly caused by Respondent's withdrawal of accommodation. *Suders*, 542 U.S. at 147.

**April 11, 2025 – HR Recording Admissions.** The April 11, 2025 HR recording (CAST-REC-0001) documents HR acknowledging procedural failures in the accommodation process. Respondent's characterization of voluntary leave is contradicted by its own contemporaneous internal directive: "Place him back on leave." Passive voice reveals agency—Respondent placed Charging Party on leave; he did not place himself.

**Respondent's Defense Fails as Matter of Law.** Respondent claims (Page 4): "On November 22, 2024, Babchuk sent a draft Coaching Memo to Employee Relations" and argues this "negat[es] any causal inference."

This argument fails because:
1. **Preparation ≠ Delivery.** The adverse action is delivery, not drafting.
2. **The decision to proceed was made AFTER the December 2 FMLA filing.** Respondent admits (Pages 4-5): "On December 2, 2024, Babchuk received an automatic notification from Sedgwick...Babchuk consulted with Employee Relations about whether to proceed."

Respondent knew about the FMLA filing. Respondent chose to proceed anyway. The 24-hour gap between FMLA filing and coaching memo delivery, combined with admitted intervening consultation, establishes direct causation under 29 U.S.C. § 2615(a)(2). *Kasten v. Saint-Gobain Performance Plastics Corp.*, 563 U.S. 1, 14 (2011).
